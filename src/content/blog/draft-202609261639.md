### Part 1 9 月在考新题（11 道）

#### Part 1 - 1. Travelling

**Q:** What transport do you often use when travelling?

**A:** I normally take high-speed trains for journeys between cities because they are punctual and
far less stressful than driving. Within a city, I tend to rely on the metro.

**Q:** Do you often ride a bicycle?

**A:** I do, although mainly at weekends. Cycling is ideal for short distances, and it lets me get
some exercise without setting aside extra time for a workout.

**Q:** Do you prefer to sit by the window when travelling?

**A:** Yes, definitely. A window seat gives me something to look at, and watching the scenery change
makes a long journey feel considerably shorter.

**Q:** Did you have a long journey with your family as a child?

**A:** Yes. When I was about ten, my family took an overnight train to Xi’an. The journey felt like
an adventure because we played cards, shared snacks and woke up in a completely different landscape.

#### Part 1 - 2. Rubbish and recycling

**Q:** Did you recycle when you were a kid?

**A:** Only in a fairly basic way. My primary school collected waste paper, so I used to save old
exercise books and put them in a large recycling box in the corridor.

**Q:** Is waste sorting common in your country?

**A:** It is becoming common, especially in major cities, although people’s habits are still uneven.
Clearer labels and regular public campaigns have made sorting much easier than it used to be.

**Q:** How do you recycle things like paper and plastic?

**A:** I flatten cardboard boxes, rinse plastic containers and place them in the appropriate bins. I
also keep reusable bags by the door so that I do not need a new plastic bag every time I shop.

**Q:** What do you do when you see rubbish on the street?

**A:** If it is safe and hygienic to do so, I pick it up and put it in the nearest bin. I would feel
rather hypocritical complaining about litter while walking straight past it.

#### Part 1 - 3. Tiredness

**Q:** What do you do when you feel tired?

**A:** I usually step away from my screen, drink some water and take a ten-minute walk. If I am
genuinely exhausted, a short nap is far more effective than another cup of coffee.

**Q:** When would you feel tired?

**A:** I feel most tired after several hours of concentrated study or after travelling all day.
Mental fatigue can be just as draining as physical exercise.

**Q:** Do you often feel tired?

**A:** Not particularly. I keep a fairly regular sleep schedule, although I do feel worn out near
exam deadlines when several tasks pile up at once.

**Q:** When you feel tired, do you still go out with your friends?

**A:** It depends on the plan. I would still meet a close friend for a quiet meal, but I would turn
down a noisy late-night event and arrange something for another day.

#### Part 1 - 4. Shoes

**Q:** Which do you prefer, fashionable shoes or comfortable shoes?

**A:** Comfortable shoes, without question. A fashionable pair is pointless if it gives me blisters
after twenty minutes, although I still prefer simple designs that look neat.

**Q:** How much money do you usually spend on shoes?

**A:** Usually between 400 and 700 yuan for a pair I can wear regularly. I would rather buy one
durable pair than replace several cheap ones.

**Q:** Have you ever bought shoes online?

**A:** Yes, but only from brands whose sizing I already know. I read the return policy carefully
because even shoes in the same stated size can fit differently.

**Q:** Do you like buying shoes? How often?

**A:** I do not regard it as a hobby, so I shop for shoes perhaps three or four times a year. I
normally wait until an old pair is worn out or I need something for a specific occasion.

#### Part 1 - 5. Politeness

**Q:** What did you do to show your politeness as a child?

**A:** I greeted adults properly, said please and thank you, and waited until other people had
finished speaking. Those small habits were my parents’ way of teaching me to respect others.

**Q:** On what occasions should we be polite?

**A:** We should be polite in almost every social situation, but it matters particularly when we
disagree with someone or speak to service staff. Courtesy is most revealing when we are frustrated.

**Q:** Do you think being polite is very important?

**A:** Yes, because politeness reduces unnecessary friction and makes people feel acknowledged. It
does not mean being artificial; it simply means expressing yourself without disregarding other
people.

**Q:** How did you learn to be polite as a child?

**A:** Mostly through example and gentle correction. My parents modelled polite behaviour, and if I
interrupted a guest or forgot to thank someone, they quietly reminded me afterwards.

#### Part 1 - 6. Fruit and vegetables

**Q:** Do people in your country like planting vegetables?

**A:** Many do, particularly older people who have a balcony or a small plot of land. They often
grow spring onions, chillies or tomatoes because these plants are useful and relatively easy to
maintain.

**Q:** Were there any kind of fruits and vegetables you disliked as a child?

**A:** I disliked bitter melon and celery when I was little because their flavours seemed
overpowering. My taste has changed, though, and I now appreciate both when they are cooked well.

**Q:** What kind of fruits and vegetables do you dislike?

**A:** I am still not keen on overly soft okra because of its texture. Apart from that, I eat almost
every common fruit and vegetable.

**Q:** Where do you usually buy fruit and vegetables?

**A:** I usually buy them from a supermarket near my apartment, as the produce is labelled clearly
and turns over quickly. At weekends, I sometimes visit a local wet market for fresher seasonal
items.

**Q:** How often do you eat fruit and vegetables?

**A:** Every day. I normally have fruit with breakfast and make sure lunch and dinner each contain
at least one vegetable, so it has become an automatic part of my routine.

#### Part 1 - 7. Advertisement

**Q:** Do you often see advertisements when you are on your phone or computer?

**A:** Constantly. Most free apps and video platforms insert adverts into the feed, so I probably
see dozens without consciously registering all of them.

**Q:** What kind of advertising do you like?

**A:** I like advertising that is concise, visually inventive and genuinely informative. A clever
story is welcome, but it should still tell me what the product actually does.

**Q:** Do you like advertisements?

**A:** Only occasionally. A well-made advert can be entertaining, whereas repetitive pop-ups and
exaggerated claims make me distrust the brand behind them.

**Q:** Do you see a lot of advertising on trains or other transport?

**A:** Yes. Digital screens in metro stations and carriages display a steady stream of adverts,
although I usually notice them only when the design is unusually striking.

**Q:** Is there an advertisement that made an impression on you when you were a child?

**A:** A public-service advert about saving water stayed with me. It showed a tap slowly draining an
entire lake, which was a simple image but a remarkably effective one.

**Q:** Have you ever bought something because of an advertisement?

**A:** Yes. I once bought a pair of noise-cancelling headphones after seeing a detailed
demonstration online. I still compared independent reviews first, so the advert sparked my interest
rather than making the decision for me.

#### Part 1 - 8. Paper

**Q:** Do you carry paper and pens with you when you go out?

**A:** I carry a small notebook and a pen, even though most of my notes are digital. Writing by hand
is quicker when I need to sketch an idea or remember something immediately.

**Q:** Do people still keep handwritten letters today?

**A:** Yes, especially letters connected with important relationships or milestones. A handwritten
message feels personal in a way that a screenshot of a text conversation rarely does.

**Q:** Do you still write physical letters?

**A:** Very rarely. I write cards for birthdays and major festivals, but for ordinary communication
I use email or instant messaging because it is faster and easier to organise.

**Q:** What did you like to do with paper as a child?

**A:** I loved folding paper planes and drawing imaginary maps. I could spend an entire afternoon
testing different designs to see which plane flew the farthest.

**Q:** Have you made any crafts with paper?

**A:** Yes. At school I made paper lanterns and a surprisingly complicated model bridge. They were
not perfect, but the process taught me to follow instructions patiently.

#### Part 1 - 9. Secondary school

**Q:** Is there anything you miss about your secondary school?

**A:** I miss the sense of shared routine. My closest friends and I saw one another every day, so
even an ordinary lunch break could become memorable.

**Q:** What was your favourite subject at secondary school?

**A:** English was my favourite subject because it opened a window onto films, books and ideas from
other cultures. I also enjoyed the freedom of expressing one thought in several different ways.

**Q:** Were there any subjects that you found difficult at secondary school?

**A:** Physics was difficult at first, particularly problems involving electricity. Once I stopped
memorising formulas and began drawing diagrams, it became much more manageable.

**Q:** Do you remember your first day at secondary school?

**A:** Quite clearly. I was nervous about finding my classroom, but another new student asked me for
directions even though I was equally lost, and that awkward moment started a long friendship.

#### Part 1 - 10. Name

**Q:** Do you often forget people’s names?

**A:** I occasionally do, especially when I meet several people at the same event. I normally
remember the face and context but need a moment to retrieve the actual name.

**Q:** How do you feel when people can’t remember your name?

**A:** I do not take it personally, because I make the same mistake myself. I simply introduce
myself again rather than allowing the situation to become uncomfortable.

**Q:** How do you remember people’s names?

**A:** I repeat the name naturally during the conversation and connect it with one distinctive
detail about the person. That association is much stronger than trying to memorise the sound alone.

**Q:** Is it easy for you to remember people’s names?

**A:** Reasonably easy if I speak to the person for a few minutes. It is much harder when names are
read rapidly from a list with no faces or stories attached to them.

#### Part 1 - 11. Opportunities

**Q:** Do you often get new opportunities?

**A:** Fairly often, mainly through university projects and student events. The opportunities are
not always dramatic, but even a small presentation or workshop can lead to useful experience.

**Q:** Do you like taking on new opportunities?

**A:** Yes, provided they fit my priorities. New opportunities keep life from becoming predictable,
although I have learned not to accept everything and then perform poorly because I am overstretched.

**Q:** Should people try as many different things as they can?

**A:** People should experiment widely when they are young, but not for the sake of constant
novelty. Trying different things is valuable when they reflect afterwards and identify what
genuinely suits them.

**Q:** Did you have any opportunities to take part in new activities as a child?

**A:** Yes. My school offered a robotics club, and joining it was my first experience of building
something as a team. Our robot barely completed the course, but I became fascinated by technology.

### Part 2&3 9 月在考新题（28 道）

#### Part 2 - 1. 对结果开心的重要决定

**Q:** Describe a time you made an important decision and were happy with the result

- What the decision was

- Why you made the decision

- How easy it was for you to make the decision

- And explain why you were happy with the result

**A:** One important decision I made was to apply for a postgraduate programme in a city where I
knew almost nobody. I had a comfortable routine in my hometown, but the programme offered the
combination of computer science and user research that I genuinely wanted. The decision was not easy
because it meant leaving my family, paying higher living costs and accepting that I might initially
feel isolated. I dealt with it methodically: I spoke to current students, compared course modules
and wrote down the best and worst realistic outcomes. Once the evidence was in front of me, the risk
looked manageable rather than frightening. I am happy with the result because the move made me far
more independent and introduced me to people with very different ideas. More importantly, the course
confirmed that I had chosen the right field. There have been difficult weeks, of course, but even
those have taught me how to organise myself and ask for help. Looking back, the decision expanded
both my academic options and my sense of what I can handle.

##### Part 3

**Q:** Which is better, to make a decision quickly or slowly?

**A:** Neither speed is inherently better. A low-stakes, reversible choice should be made quickly,
while a decision about health, education or finance deserves evidence and consultation. The real
skill is matching the process to the consequences rather than using one pace for everything.

**Q:** Do you think most people’s decisions are made in their 20s?

**A:** Many highly visible decisions occur in people’s twenties, such as study, work and
relationships, but they are not necessarily final. Careers, families and values continue to change
throughout life. The twenties are better described as a period of frequent first decisions than the
point when an entire future is fixed.

**Q:** What if people in a group have different opinions on a decision?

**A:** They should first agree on the objective and the criteria for judging options. Each view then
needs a fair hearing, followed by a clear decision method such as consensus, a responsible leader or
a vote. Recording the reasons helps people support the outcome even if their preferred option loses.

**Q:** What little decisions do people make every day?

**A:** People choose when to wake up, what to eat, how to travel, which message to answer first and
how to spend small amounts of time and money. Each choice seems minor, but repeated patterns shape
health and productivity. Good routines reduce the number that require conscious effort.

**Q:** Do you think it is a good idea to make a small decision every day?

**A:** Not necessarily. The consequences, available evidence and time pressure differ from one
choice to another. Choosing a university deserves research, whereas choosing lunch usually relies on
habit. The qualification is that too much analysis can become a way of postponing an uncomfortable
choice.

**Q:** How can we tell the difference between an important and an easy decision?

**A:** An important decision has substantial or lasting consequences and is often difficult to
reverse. An easy decision may still matter, but the cost of being wrong is low. I would judge the
difference by impact, reversibility and how many other people are affected.

#### Part 2 - 2. 做困难事情并成功的人

**Q:** Describe a person you know who did something difficult and was successful

- Who this person is

- What difficult thing this person did

- Why this person was successful

- And explain how you feel about this person

**A:** The person I have in mind is my cousin Mei, who changed careers from accounting to software
design in her early thirties. She had a stable job and no formal technology background, so the
transition involved a real financial and psychological risk. For almost a year, she studied after
work, built small projects and applied for entry-level positions while receiving a steady stream of
rejections. What made her successful was not exceptional talent but a disciplined feedback loop.
After each failed interview, she identified one weakness, practised it and asked someone experienced
to review her work. Eventually, a small education company hired her, and she later became the lead
designer of one of its learning products. I admire her because she was realistic without becoming
cynical. She did not pretend that passion alone would solve everything; she saved money, set
milestones and accepted a temporary drop in status. Her experience changed my view of success. It
showed me that courage can be quiet and repetitive: sometimes it simply means returning to a
difficult task the next evening.

##### Part 3

**Q:** In which sectors are companies more likely to succeed in China?

**A:** Technology, health care, green energy, advanced manufacturing and services for an ageing
population all have strong potential. However, no sector guarantees success. A company still needs
genuine demand, sound cash flow and the ability to earn trust in a highly competitive market.

**Q:** Is it easy to start up a company?

**A:** Registration may be relatively straightforward, but building a sustainable company is
difficult. Founders must test demand, secure cash flow, recruit capable people and respond to
regulation and competition. Starting is an event; surviving long enough to earn repeat customers is
the real challenge.

**Q:** Other than making money, what is more important for a company?

**A:** A company needs a useful purpose, customer trust and the ability to retain capable employees.
Profit keeps it alive, but those qualities explain why people choose it repeatedly. Environmental
and social responsibility also protect the firm’s long-term licence to operate.

**Q:** Does money equal success?

**A:** No. Money provides security and expands choice, so it is an important part of success, but it
cannot measure health, integrity, relationships or social value. I would call a business successful
when it is financially sustainable and creates something worthwhile without damaging the people or
environment around it.

**Q:** How can companies help protect the environment?

**A:** A practical approach is to test demand on a small scale, manage costs and build environmental
responsibility into operations. A neighbourhood bakery can outperform a fashionable start-up by
serving regular customers reliably. This works best when people understand the purpose and receive
enough support to act consistently.

**Q:** Is it difficult to do business in today’s world?

**A:** Yes, because competition is global, customer expectations change quickly and one online
failure can damage trust at scale. At the same time, digital tools have lowered the cost of reaching
a market. Starting is more accessible, but building a resilient and responsible company remains
difficult.

#### Part 2 - 3. 快乐人士

**Q:** Describe a happy person you know

- Who this person is

- What he/she is like

- How he/she shows happiness

- And explain why you think he/she is a happy person

**A:** A genuinely happy person I know is my grandmother. She is in her seventies and has a calm,
curious personality. She notices small pleasures that the rest of us often rush past, such as the
first flowers on her balcony or a perfectly ripe peach at the market. She shows happiness openly but
not dramatically. She laughs easily, sends voice messages when she discovers a new recipe and always
gives visitors her complete attention. What makes her seem deeply happy is that her mood is not
dependent on everything going smoothly. She has dealt with illness and loss, yet she does not deny
those difficulties or let them occupy every conversation. Instead, she maintains a few reliable
sources of meaning: caring for plants, walking with neighbours and keeping the family connected.
When I spend time with her, I become less preoccupied with achievement and more aware of the present
moment. To me, her happiness is not constant excitement. It is the ability to remain interested in
life and generous towards other people, even on an ordinary day.

##### Part 3

**Q:** When do you think people are happiest?

**A:** It is most common when lasting happiness usually comes from security, meaningful
relationships and a sense of progress. Situations involving friendship, purposeful work, art, rest,
play and small moments of competence make that need especially visible. However, forcing constant
cheerfulness can hide distress and make people feel guilty for normal sadness, so context should
guide the response.

**Q:** How can the arts spread positive emotions in society?

**A:** The arts create shared experiences through music, images, stories and performance. Public
concerts, community theatre and accessible exhibitions can give people relief while also helping
them feel understood. Their emotional value is greatest when participation is affordable and
reflects more than one narrow group.

**Q:** Do you think teachers are able to spot the unhappy children?

**A:** Generally, yes, although it depends on the circumstances. The main benefit is that positive
emotion improves resilience, cooperation and willingness to learn. At the same time, forcing
constant cheerfulness can hide distress and make people feel guilty for normal sadness. A balanced
approach is to create safe relationships, manageable demands and opportunities for genuine
achievement.

**Q:** Should teachers know what children are doing all the time?

**A:** Not necessarily. A student may value one trusted teacher and a sense of belonging more than
expensive facilities. More broadly, positive emotion improves resilience, cooperation and
willingness to learn; however, forcing constant cheerfulness can hide distress and make people feel
guilty for normal sadness.

**Q:** What would make a student happy at school?

**A:** The most important point is that lasting happiness usually comes from security, meaningful
relationships and a sense of progress. In practice, this may involve friendship, purposeful work,
art, rest, play and small moments of competence. For example, a student may value one trusted
teacher and a sense of belonging more than expensive facilities, although individual circumstances
still matter.

**Q:** Do you think doing a job that you like is more important than other things?

**A:** Enjoying the work is important because it supports motivation, but it cannot replace fair
pay, health, security and respectful colleagues. A fascinating job that damages the rest of life is
not sustainable. I would seek meaningful work within a package of reasonable conditions rather than
rank one factor absolutely.

#### Part 2 - 4. 看过的名人采访

**Q:** Describe a time you watched a famous person being interviewed

- Who the famous person was

- What the interview was about

- When and where you watched the interview

- And explain how you felt about the interview

**A:** I once watched a long-form interview with the retired tennis player Li Na. I found it online
during a period when I was struggling with a demanding project, and I expected a conventional
discussion of trophies and training. Instead, much of the interview concerned pressure, injury and
the loneliness of making decisions that other people may not understand. She spoke candidly about
leaving the state sports system, taking responsibility for her own career and learning from defeats
that were publicly visible. What struck me was her directness. She did not turn every setback into
an inspirational slogan; she explained the practical work and uncertainty behind her achievements. I
watched the interview alone at my desk, but I paused several times to write down ideas, particularly
her distinction between being confident and being unafraid. I came away feeling steadier rather than
simply excited. The interview reminded me that high performers are not free from doubt. They develop
routines that allow them to act despite it, which was exactly the perspective I needed at that
moment.

**A:** They should look neat, comfortable and appropriate for the role. A bank may expect formal
clothing, whereas a design studio may accept something more relaxed. The aim is to show respect and
judgement without wearing an unfamiliar costume that distracts the candidate throughout the
interview.

**A:** The main reason is that an interview reveals communication, judgement and mutual fit that a
document cannot fully show. A candidate who explains one setback thoughtfully may be more convincing
than one who claims perfection. However, bias and artificial performance can reward confidence more
than actual ability, so context and implementation still matter.

**A:** A practical approach is to research the organisation, prepare specific examples and practise
concise but honest explanations. A candidate who explains one setback thoughtfully may be more
convincing than one who claims perfection. This works best when people understand the purpose and
receive enough support to act consistently.

**Q:** Why do some people think interviews are a waste of time?

**A:** The main reason is that an interview reveals communication, judgement and mutual fit that a

**Q:** Why do some people dislike being interviewed?

**A:** They may fear judgement, unfamiliar questions or losing control over how their words are
presented. Formal interviews also demand immediate, coherent answers, which can disadvantage
thoughtful people who need time. Clear expectations and respectful follow-up questions reduce that
pressure.

**Q:** What kinds of people are usually interviewed?

**A:** Employers interview applicants, journalists interview public figures, experts and witnesses,
and researchers interview participants with relevant experience. The common feature is that spoken
answers reveal context and judgement that a form cannot capture. Ethical interviewers should make
the purpose and use of the information clear.

to him because research contains a great deal of uncertainty; a clear process prevents that
uncertainty from becoming chaos. I also appreciate that his organisation serves people rather than
controlling them. He builds time for questions and unexpected results instead of scheduling every
minute. Watching him has changed my own habits. I now prepare a short agenda before group
discussions and end them by confirming the next action, which saves everyone from having the same
conversation twice.

#### Part 2 - 6. 嘈杂地

**Q:** Describe a noisy place you have been to

- Where it is

- When you went there

- What you did there

- And explain why you feel it’s a noisy place

**A:** A particularly noisy place I visited was a night market in Chengdu during the National Day
holiday. I went with two school friends whom I had not seen for months. The market occupied several
narrow streets, and every few metres a vendor was calling out prices, a speaker was playing music or
oil was crackling on a grill. On top of that, thousands of visitors were talking at once, so we had
to lean towards one another just to hold a basic conversation. We sampled spicy noodles, watched a
craftsman make sugar figures and slowly moved through the crowd. The noise came from many
overlapping sources rather than one unbearable sound, which made the place feel both exhausting and
alive. After about an hour, I needed a quiet side street to reset my senses. I would not want to
live beside that market, but I was glad to experience it. In that context, the noise was part of the
atmosphere: it signalled appetite, trade and celebration, even though it also reminded me how
quickly sensory excitement can turn into fatigue.

##### Part 3

**Q:** How can people consider others’ feelings when chatting in public?

**A:** A practical approach is to lower voices in shared spaces, use headphones responsibly and
enforce time and place limits. Conversation is welcome in a cafe but disruptive in a hospital
corridor or quiet carriage. This works best when people understand the purpose and receive enough
support to act consistently.

**Q:** How would people usually respond to noises in your country?

**A:** A practical approach is to lower voices in shared spaces, use headphones responsibly and
enforce time and place limits. Conversation is welcome in a cafe but disruptive in a hospital
corridor or quiet carriage. This works best when people understand the purpose and receive enough
support to act consistently.

**Q:** Which area is exposed to noise more, the city or the countryside?

**A:** Cities are generally noisier because traffic, construction and dense commercial activity
continue for much of the day. Rural areas have machinery, animals and occasional loud events, but
the sound is usually less constant. Duration and lack of control often matter more than the maximum
volume.

**Q:** What kinds of noises are there in our life?

##### Part 3

**Q:** How can parents help children develop a love of reading?

**A:** A practical approach is to let children choose appealing books, read together and make books
easy to reach. A child who sees adults read for pleasure is more likely to view books as part of
life. This works best when people understand the purpose and receive enough support to act
consistently.

**Q:** Why do some people prefer watching movies to reading books?

**A:** Movies require less sustained effort and combine image, sound and performance into an
immediate experience. They are also easy to share socially and can be completed in one sitting.
Books offer greater depth, but that depth demands time and active imagination that tired people may
not have.

**Q:** Do you think people today read less than before? Why?

**A:** People may read fewer long books, but they process enormous amounts of text through messages,
articles and social media. The deeper change is fragmented attention: digital reading encourages
rapid switching and makes sustained concentration harder. So the quantity is unclear, but the form
and depth have certainly changed.

**Q:** Are success books useful to everyone?

**A:** No. A good book may provide structure or a useful example, but universal formulas often
ignore luck, resources and different definitions of success. Readers should treat such books as one
perspective to test, not a substitute for evidence or personal judgement.

**Q:** Do you think people should read the original book before watching the movie?

**A:** It is not a requirement. Reading first preserves the chance to imagine the world
independently, whereas watching first can make a complex book more approachable. I prefer the book
first when possible, but the more important step is comparing how each medium makes different
choices.

**Q:** What kinds of books are popular in China?

**A:** Typical examples include fiction, history, self-development, science, biography, online
literature and practical guides. Their importance varies with age, resources and personal goals. The
common feature is that reading develops sustained attention by requiring the mind to build
connections beyond the visible page.

##### Part 3

**Q:** Should children be taught to be kind to others?

**A:** Generally, yes, although it depends on the circumstances. A child can prepare the table or
explain a new phone feature without taking over an adult’s role. More broadly, practical help builds
trust and teaches children that they can contribute to shared life; however, unasked-for help or
constant rescue can create dependence and resentment.

**Q:** Should people be very kind when they help others?

**A:** Generally, yes, although it depends on the circumstances. Kindness becomes valuable when it
respects the other person’s agency rather than displaying the helper. A child can prepare the table
or explain a new phone feature without taking over an adult’s role. The qualification is that
unasked-for help or constant rescue can create dependence and resentment.

**Q:** Can kids provide any help to parents?

**A:** Generally, yes, although it depends on the circumstances. The main benefit is that practical
help builds trust and teaches children that they can contribute to shared life. At the same time,
unasked-for help or constant rescue can create dependence and resentment. A balanced approach is to
ask what is needed, offer a manageable action and let the recipient retain control.

**Q:** Should parents give children advice? Why?

**A:** Yes, because children lack experience of some long-term consequences, but advice should
support judgement rather than replace it. Parents can explain options, risks and values, then allow
age-appropriate decisions. Constant instruction without listening may simply teach children to hide
their choices.

**Q:** What kind of advice should parents give to their children?

**A:** Parents should offer age-appropriate advice about safety, relationships, money, study habits
and long-term consequences. They should explain their reasoning and leave room for the child to
decide when the risk is manageable. Advice becomes less useful when it is really disguised control.

**Q:** Should parents help their kids with their homework?

**A:** They should provide a quiet environment, clarify instructions and help the child plan, but
they should not complete the work. Homework is useful only if the teacher can see what the student
understands. Productive help gradually makes the child more independent.
